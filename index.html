Diego Guevara · c. 1870–1939

Stop 01 · 1870–1914

Empires & Rivalries

Key developments

  1. c. 1870–1914

    The Second Industrial Revolution expanded production through steel, electricity, and oil. Industrial power helped reshape global competition and imperial expansion, as the course slides describe.

  2. 1884–1885

    European powers met at the Berlin Conference to establish rules concerning territorial claims and trade in Africa. In my response, I emphasized that their priorities were their own expansion and competition rather than the interests of African communities.

  3. Before 1914

    My response identifies competition over colonies, resources, trade routes, and political influence as tensions that developed before World War I.

Key concept

Imperialism

A powerful country extends control over other territories and peoples to gain resources, influence, or strategic advantages.

Source spotlight · primary source

A colonial map and its silences

The map of European claims in Africa used in my assignment presents Africa through borders and European territorial claims. As I wrote, it “captures European ambitions while ignoring the people most affected: Africans themselves.” An African ruler’s account or a map of African kingdoms would offer a different perspective.

Looking back · earlier assignment

Take-Home Assignment: Reading Primary Sources Against the “Age of Total War”

In this assignment, I examined European competition and the exclusion of African voices. I would keep that argument. I would put more emphasis on the limits of the source, because a map of European claims does not show how Africans understood or resisted those claims.

Stop 02 · 1914–1918

World War I

Key developments

  1. 1914

    World War I began. In my Berlin Conference response, I described 1914 as both an endpoint for earlier rivalries and a break in the scale of conflict.

  2. 1914–1918

    My response discusses industrial weapons, massive armies, and the involvement of civilians and entire societies. The war grew from earlier tensions while changing the scale and way war was fought.

  3. 1918

    The armistice ended the fighting in November. The war’s financial and political effects carried into the postwar years, as I later discussed in my hyperinflation response.

Key concept

Total war

War mobilizing an entire society, including its economy, industry, and civilians, to support fighting.

Source spotlight · later representation

All Quiet on the Western Front

In the 2022 German film, uniforms collected from dead soldiers are washed, repaired, and issued again. I argued that this cycle makes the soldier seem disposable. The final attack also challenges the idea that war produces progress or rewards the strongest: the deaths serve power and national pride rather than the soldiers’ survival. This film represents World War I from a later period; it is not contemporary footage.

Looking back · earlier assignment

Social Darwinism, War, and All Quiet on the Western Front

In this assignment, I argued that the recycled uniforms and final attack show soldiers being treated as disposable. I still agree with that argument. These scenes support my point that survival in war does not necessarily prove someone is stronger or more deserving.

Stop 03 · 1919–1920s

New Orders

Key developments

  1. 1919–1920

    The Paris Peace Conference and the Treaty of Versailles reshaped the postwar settlement. The League of Nations was established in 1920 to provide a forum for international disputes, though the United States did not join. These arrangements aimed to build a new order after the war.

  2. Early 1920s

    I examined German banknotes with denominations of 20 million and 5 billion marks. Their large numbers did not mean people had become richer; they showed money losing its purchasing power. The banknotes alone do not explain the political and economic causes.

  3. 1925

    The Great Gatsby was published. My essay distinguishes publication in 1925 from the story’s 1922 setting and the crash in 1929. I argued that Fitzgerald explored problems within the culture of prosperity rather than predicting a particular crash.

Key concept

Hyperinflation

Extremely rapid price increases that reduce money’s purchasing power and make everyday goods increasingly difficult to afford.

Source spotlight · primary sources

Banknotes and an advertisement

The German banknotes in my crisis-of-liberalism assignment made my notes about economic insecurity more concrete. I contrasted them with an Esslinger’s advertisement in a Philadelphia 1930 phone book. The banknotes showed declining currency value but did not, by themselves, explain its causes. The advertisement showed what a business wanted consumers to believe, not conditions experienced by everyone.

Looking back · earlier assignment

Take-Home Assignment: From Revolutionary Possibilities to the Crisis of Liberalism

In this assignment, I compared the German banknotes with an advertisement promising quality and prosperity. I would keep my comparison, but make the difference in timing clearer. The banknotes come from the early 1920s, while the advertisement is from 1930, so they show different situations rather than one shared moment.

Stop 04 · 1929–1939

Fall of Liberalism

Key developments

  1. October 24, 1929

    Black Thursday marked a stock market crash. My Gatsby essay discusses panic selling and the risks of buying stocks with borrowed money: borrowing increased gains while prices rose but left investors exposed when prices fell.

  2. 1932–1933

    Roosevelt won the U.S. presidency in 1932 and launched the New Deal in 1933 for relief, recovery, and regulation. My essay contrasts Gatsby’s pursuit of personal wealth with recovery treated as a public responsibility. In Germany, Hitler was appointed chancellor on January 30, 1933; the Nazi regime then suspended civil liberties and attacked political opponents.

  3. September 1, 1939

    Germany invaded Poland, beginning World War II in Europe. This is where my roadmap ends.

Key concept

Fascism

An authoritarian movement placing nation and powerful leader above individual rights, suppressing opposition, and promoting extreme nationalism and militarism.

Source spotlight · reading and primary source

Insecurity and political confidence

In my crisis-of-liberalism response, I used Hobsbawm’s “The Fall of Liberalism” to discuss the relationship between insecurity and political confidence. I stressed that dictatorship was not guaranteed by hyperinflation. I also identified the Great Depression, fear of revolution, and support for authoritarian leaders as factors discussed in the reading. The 1930 Esslinger’s advertisement shows a business’s promise to consumers, not everyone’s lived conditions.

Looking back · earlier assignment

The Green Light and the Crash

In this essay, I argued that visible wealth and confidence do not guarantee stability. I would keep that argument. I would also keep the distinction that Gatsby helps explain the culture surrounding prosperity, but the novel does not predict or fully explain the crash.

Across the stops

Three cause-and-effect connections

  1. Empire → war

    In my Berlin Conference response, I argued that competition over colonies, resources, trade, and influence contributed to tensions before 1914. The war grew out of those rivalries but changed the scale of conflict.

  2. War → postwar insecurity

    My hyperinflation response connects wartime debt and reparations with postwar financial problems, alongside politics and money printing. The effects of war continued after the fighting ended.

  3. Prosperity → crisis

    My Gatsby essay links the confidence and financial weaknesses of the 1920s to exposure when prices fell in 1929, then connects the crisis to a larger government role through the New Deal.

Moments of change

Two turning points

1914 · An endpoint and a breaking point

This source makes me view 1914 as both an endpoint and a breaking point. On one hand, the Berlin Conference shows how imperialism, nationalism, and European rivalries had been building up for years before World War I. Countries were already competing over land, colonies, resources, trade, and influence. But 1914 is also a real turning point, the moment when those old tensions exploded into something far worse. World War I increased the scale of the conflict through industrial weapons, massive armies, and the involvement of civilians and entire societies. The war grew out of those earlier rivalries, but it changed everything, becoming what Hobsbawm describes as “total war.”

1929 · Confidence gives way

I chose the 1929 crash because it changed people’s confidence in economic prosperity, and it opened the possibility of a larger government role in relief and recovery through programs such as the New Deal. In my Gatsby essay, I discussed how crowds could spread confidence but also fear. Borrowing to buy stocks increased the risks when prices fell, and apparent wealth did not guarantee security. My essay also distinguished the crash from the whole Great Depression: a single day of falling prices could not explain the entire disaster. The New Deal discussion helped me consider recovery as a public responsibility, while recognizing that relief could still leave inequalities in place.

Looking across the roadmap

Reflection

What surprised me when seeing these four responses together was how often I questioned whether something that looked powerful or successful actually helped ordinary people. The colonial map showed European claims while leaving African perspectives out. The recycled uniforms in All Quiet on the Western Front showed the human cost behind military glory. Large banknote denominations did not mean people were wealthy, and Gatsby’s expensive parties did not mean his life was secure.

My Berlin Conference response already showed a change in my thinking. I moved from simply listing imperialism as a cause of World War I to seeing 1914 as both continuity and a break. Earlier rivalries continued, but the scale and way war was fought changed. Comparing the advertisement and banknotes also made my general notes about opportunity and exclusion more concrete. More production or more money on paper did not automatically mean that people felt safer.

I understand the limits of evidence more clearly now. Advertisements show what businesses wanted people to believe, while maps and banknotes leave important perspectives or causes unexplained. In my Gatsby essay, I also distinguished an analogy from an explanation. The novel helps me discuss the culture of prosperity, but it does not explain every cause of the crash. These limits do not make the sources useless; they make me more careful about what I claim from them.

Another idea I would keep is that outcomes were not inevitable. Economic insecurity did not guarantee dictatorship. As we move into World War II, I want to ask why some countries responded to economic and political insecurity through reform, while others moved toward dictatorship and expansion, and how those different choices shaped the outbreak of the war.
